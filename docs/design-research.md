# Dad With The Dagger — creator-site research and practical design/content brief

Research captured: **2026-10-07 UTC**. Research only: no site, repository, DNS, accounts, forms, or commercial records were changed.

## 1. The five strongest decisions

These are **recommendations**, not claims that a particular layout has proven conversion results.

1. **Build a personal resource, not a fitness-business funnel.** Make the first screen identify James and the help available, then lead to useful food content. Ben Carpenter demonstrates a focused identity and one dominant action; Bored of Lunch and The Meal Prep Manual demonstrate clear food-discovery routes.[61][56][59] Borrow that clarity, not a newsletter gate, coaching application, or product bundle as the main destination.
2. **Let real-life specificity establish trust.** Use a real portrait, an approved short bio, and concrete details James is comfortable sharing about working, parenting, and trying to make weight loss manageable. Lillie explicitly distinguishes her lived experience from dietitian/coaching credentials; Nathan Anthony explains the full-time-job context behind his cooking project.[62][73] Do not substitute invented pounds lost, client results, certifications, follower counts, or media logos.
3. **Make recipes and the signature sauce the useful core.** Start with a small, completely real library rather than a large-looking mock catalog. Skinnytaste exposes recipe time, nutrition, author, and jump/print controls; The Meal Prep Manual gives portion-based information; Lillie treats sauce as a concrete component of an actual meal.[68][66][69] Borrow the information structure, never their recipes and photographs disguised as James's.
4. **Use restrained editorial styling, with food and James doing the visual work.** The inspected sites range from Ben's high-contrast black/gradient identity to Sohee's coral/navy quiz presentation and Bored of Lunch's bright illustrated kitchen world.[61][41][56] For James, choose legible type, a warm neutral background, one muted accent, real food photography, and a modest portrait. Avoid bodybuilding drama, giant transformation claims, forced jokes, and a dominant weapon/warrior aesthetic.
5. **Make HTLT an optional, plainly disclosed recommendation.** Keep the affiliate block below substantive personal/food content. Put the financial disclosure immediately beside the link, not only in a footer. The FTC expressly distinguishes an understandable commission disclosure from the ambiguous label “affiliate link” and warns that a personalized discount code may not communicate the financial relationship.[32] Preserve the exact user-supplied link and code; do not invent products James uses or promise the offer has been checkout-tested.

### Why a typical influencer template would clash

The sample includes coaching funnels with professional/social proof, program sales, cookbook bundles, and celebrity/media routing.[1][41][60] Those patterns serve businesses with products, credentials, and established audiences. **Recommendation:** do not make James look as though he offers individualized coaching, sells a transformation system, or already has an extensive commercial operation.

Avoid these motifs unless James specifically supplies the authentic content and wants them:

- Shirtless physique hero, before/after slider, or cropped body-as-proof imagery.
- “No excuses,” “beast mode,” “destroy fat,” “unlock your potential,” or guaranteed-results copy.
- Military stencil/condensed type, neon gym colors, distressed textures, weapon silhouettes, and large aggressive dagger imagery.
- Fake media-logo strips, testimonial cards, follower counters, star ratings, “trusted by” numbers, and animated progress statistics.
- Countdown offers, cart icons, product grids, and repetitive discount banners.
- Fitness jargon, diet labels, and macro targets presented as a required identity.

Motivation should come from a clear next action—read a useful note, make a real meal, try a confirmed sauce—not from a slogan about discipline.

## 5. Practical page and content brief

Everything in this section is a **proposed specification**, not an account of content James already has.

### Homepage order and navigation

**Header:** modest Dad With The Dagger wordmark; visible links **About · Recipes · Signature Sauce · HTLT**. A social link to the verified account can sit quietly in the header or footer. Use section anchors initially if the content is compact; give real recipes their own shareable pages when available.

| Order | Section | Purpose and content | CTA | Publication gate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Introduction | Name, regular-working-dad context, what visitors can find, real clothed portrait | **Browse recipes**; quieter **Meet James** | If there are no approved recipes, make Meet James primary instead. |
| 2 | About James | Short approved story: work/family context, why this matters, what he wants to share, boundaries | Read more only if there is genuinely more | No invented timeline, weight history, credentials, or family details. |
| 3 | Recipes | A small set of actual dishes, large enough photographs, useful metadata | View recipe | Exact tested recipe and image permission required. |
| 4 | Signature Sauce | A distinctive editorial feature: actual sauce photo, what it tastes like, how it is used | Get the recipe | James must supply/approve ingredients, quantities, method, yield, and storage guidance. |

| 6 | Footer | Verified social/contact destination, affiliate-disclosure detail, privacy information appropriate to actual tools used | Direct, descriptive links | No fake contact form, mailing list, or unsupported privacy promises. |

Do not publish a wall of convincing-looking placeholders. If all four subject areas are required at launch, collect the real content first. A short truthful page is better than a simulated established creator business.

### Safe draft positioning copy

Subject to James's approval:

> **Hi, I'm James. A working dad sharing practical food and weight-loss ideas.**
>
> This is where I'm bringing together recipes, my signature sauce, and straightforward notes on products and habits.

The second sentence is appropriate only when those resources genuinely exist on the site. Do not imply that he has already tested a habit, developed a recipe library, or achieved a particular result just to make the paragraph smoother.

Possible section headings: **About James · Recipes · Signature Sauce · HTLT**. These are deliberately descriptive. No slogan is necessary. Use “What I use” only after an actual product-use list has been confirmed.

### Bio structure and trust boundaries

Aim for one short homepage paragraph and an optional longer story, not a résumé impersonating a coach:

1. Who James is, using approved wording.
2. Why he cares about weight loss and helping others, in his words.
3. The real constraints around work/family that he chooses to disclose.
4. What he shares here, and what he does not claim to provide.
5. One genuine personal detail, if he wants it, to make the voice recognizable.

Do not fill missing facts with “busy father of two,” “lost X pounds,” “transformed my life,” specific work shifts, a training history, medical conditions, or certifications. A scope line can say the site shares personal experience and general information; that is not a substitute for accurate content or a license to make medical claims.

### Recipe card and recipe page

**Browse card:** actual image; literal recipe name; brief practical description; total time and servings; optional clearly labeled estimated calories/protein if supported. Do not show zeroes when values are unknown.

**Recipe page:**

- A short human introduction about why James makes it, only if true.
- A visible **Jump to recipe** link if there is substantial introductory material, and a clean **Print recipe** option.
- Prep/active time and total/elapsed time distinguished; servings and a meaningful portion definition.
- Exact ingredients with units, necessary equipment, and numbered steps.
- Tested substitutions, relevant allergens, and storage/reheating information that has been responsibly checked. Do not invent a safe shelf life.
- Nutrition as an estimate with portion basis, ingredient assumptions, and calculation source; recalculate when the recipe changes.
- Credit/link for an adaptation when appropriate. Other creators' recipes may be clearly linked as recommendations, not relabeled as James's original work.
- No fabricated rating stars, comments, save counts, or “reader favorite” labels.

For a small library, begin with a simple list or grid. Add only useful categories supported by the actual inventory—such as quick meals, meal prep, or sauces. Do not launch elaborate dietary filters, search sliders, or empty category pages to imitate a mature site.

### Signature sauce: make it a useful feature, not a fictional product

Treat **Signature Sauce** as a working section label until James confirms its actual name and details. The feature should answer:

- What does it taste like, in ordinary words?
- What goes into it, in exact quantities?
- How much does it make, and what counts as a serving?
- How is it prepared, stored, and used safely?
- Which of James's real dishes use it?
- Are there tested alternatives for any ingredient?

A small jar/bowl photograph and a photograph of the sauce on an actual meal would tell a clearer story than a dramatic branded bottle mockup. Do not invent “secret” ingredients, bottle packaging, purchase options, “zero calorie,” “high protein,” “fat burning,” or preservation claims. If the sauce is not yet ready, keep the detailed feature in the draft rather than manufacturing a launch-ready recipe.

### HTLT product-use entries

If James supplies specific products, each entry can contain the exact product/flavor, a first-person description of its genuine use, what he likes and any limitation he has actually experienced, and the relevant link/disclosure. Do not scrape the HTLT catalog and present the resulting lineup as his personal stack.

Keep recipes usable without a supplement purchase. Avoid language making HTLT necessary for success or implying a supplement caused weight loss. Do not label a product “my daily go-to,” “family approved,” “best,” or “personally tested” without confirmation.

## 6. Visual and interaction direction

**Recommendation: a restrained personal editorial site.**

- **Palette:** warm off-white background, near-black/charcoal text, one muted rust or warm green accent. Validate actual contrast during implementation. HTLT's branding should not become the whole site's visual identity.
- **Typography:** a legible system/body font and a calm, strong heading style. Use sentence case. Avoid all-caps motivational walls and overly condensed gym-poster type.
- **Photography:** one genuine, clothed portrait of James in an ordinary setting; original food photos with consistent light/crops. No stock athlete, AI before/after image, or borrowed meal presented as his cooking. Do not use identifiable family photos without appropriate permission.
- **Layout:** comfortable spacing, readable text widths, a modest header, and clear hierarchy. Use food photographs for interest instead of decorative icon grids, endless badges, and exaggerated brand marks.
- **Mobile:** maintain a direct route from the introduction to real food. Keep the affiliate disclosure and button in the same visual component. Prefer a few clearly named actions over floating social/favorite/search controls. The Skinnytaste capture demonstrates how many individually useful controls can consume a mobile first screen.[68]
- **Accessibility:** descriptive links, visible keyboard focus, labelled controls, useful image alt text, readable contrast, and no hover-only information. Any motion should be optional; no autoplay motivational video.
- **Static-first scope:** favor static content and simple navigation suitable for the intended hosting approach. Do not add an account system, cart, comments, newsletter signup, or contact form unless a real service and maintenance plan exist. This research did not audit hosting-policy eligibility, domain ownership, DNS, or implementation security.

## 7. FTC-based affiliate disclosure brief

This is implementation guidance based on the FTC's first-party publications, **not a legal opinion about any sampled creator or a guarantee of compliance**.

### What the guidance actually says

- The FTC explains that financial and other material brand relationships should be disclosed, and the disclosure should be placed with the endorsement itself where people will see and understand it—not only in an About page, at the end, or behind an extra click.[31]
- For affiliate websites, the FTC gives the example “I get commissions for purchases made through links in this post,” and emphasizes proximity between the recommendation, link, and disclosure.[32]
- The FTC says that “affiliate link” alone may not tell consumers the publisher gets paid; a buy-now button is also not an adequate disclosure by itself.[32]
- A personalized discount code may convey some relationship without making the financial relationship clear. When consumer understanding is uncertain, the FTC recommends erring on the side of disclosure.[32]
- Endorsements must reflect real experience: the FTC specifically says not to discuss experience with a product not tried, misrepresent an opinion, or make unsupported product claims.[31]

### Recommended HTLT component

Place the disclosure **above or immediately beside** the outbound CTA in normal, readable text:

> **Affiliate disclosure:** I'm an HTLT affiliate. I earn a commission on qualifying purchases made through this link.
>
> **Code: DADDAGGER — 15% off**
>
> **Visit HTLT**

Link target: `https://www.htltsupps.com?sca_ref=10886340.d8RxKeL1BQC`

This is proposed copy using the supplied affiliate relationship and offer. Confirm the actual commission arrangement, current discount eligibility, exclusions, and any expiry before publication. The offer was **not** validated by checkout in this research. Do not assume that visiting the link automatically applies the code, that the code applies to every item, or that it stacks with other offers.

A separate footer disclosure page can add details, but should not be the only place visitors learn about commission. Repeat an appropriate short disclosure wherever a distinct recommendation or link would otherwise appear without that context. If a specific product was gifted or sponsored, disclose that actual relationship too. Avoid adding “at no extra cost to you” unless that statement has been confirmed.

Do not use a generic “results vary” or “not medical advice” line as a repair for an unsupported supplement/weight-loss promise. The FTC explains that exceptional-result endorsements can imply typical results, and a generic variation disclaimer does not necessarily change that interpretation.[32]
